If you are starting flight training in Australia, two terms you will come across early are **RPL** and **RPC**.

They sound similar. They both relate to recreational flying. They can both be valid starting points. They can both lead to a lot of fun in aviation.

But they are not the same thing.

The **RPL**, or **Recreational Pilot Licence**, is a CASA flight crew licence. The **RPC**, or **Recreational Pilot Certificate**, is issued through Recreational Aviation Australia, commonly known as **RAAus**. That difference matters, because each pathway sits in a different system and can lead to different privileges, aircraft, medical requirements, costs, and future options.

This article is not about saying one is better than the other. It is about helping you understand which one might suit your goals.

## The short version

The RPC sits inside the RAAus recreational aviation system. The RPL sits inside the CASA flight crew licensing system. That is the core distinction, and most of the practical differences flow from it.

If you want a simple recreational pathway in lighter aircraft, often at lower cost, the RPC can be a great place to start. If you want to train in the CASA system, fly heavier general aviation aircraft, or move more directly toward a PPL or CPL, the RPL may make more sense.

The line between the two is not rigid. Many pilots start with an RPC and later transition into the CASA system. Others begin directly with an RPL. Some are perfectly happy staying within RAAus for their entire flying lives.

The right choice depends on what you want to do with flying.

## What is an RPC?

An RPC is a Recreational Pilot Certificate issued through Recreational Aviation Australia.

This pathway is focused on recreational flying in RAAus-registered aircraft. The aircraft are generally smaller, lighter, and cheaper to operate than traditional general aviation aircraft. Training is delivered through RAAus flight training schools, aero clubs, and recreational aviation organisations.

An RPC may suit you if you mainly want to fly for fun, keep costs as low as practical, fly light recreational aircraft, join a recreational aviation community, train at a local RAAus school or club, or start flying without immediately committing to a commercial pathway.

The RPC can also be a stepping stone. Plenty of pilots start in RAAus, gain experience, then move into the CASA system later. The hours you build in RAAus aircraft can count toward later CASA licences.

## What is an RPL?

An RPL is a Recreational Pilot Licence issued by CASA.

It allows you to fly a light, single-engine aircraft as pilot in command without supervision. To get one, you must be at least 16, meet English language requirements, pass the RPL exam, complete at least 25 hours of flying time including 20 hours dual and 5 hours as pilot in command, and pass an RPL flight test.

An RPL may suit you if you want to train in the CASA licensing system, fly general aviation aircraft, potentially continue toward a PPL or CPL, train at a GA flying school, or build experience in a more traditional CASA pathway.

The RPL is still a recreational licence. It is not a commercial qualification. But it sits neatly on the path toward higher CASA licences.

## Aircraft: what can you actually fly?

This is where the practical differences start to show up.

An RPC means flying RAAus-registered aircraft. This includes a wide range of light sport aircraft and recreational aircraft, often two-seat machines like the Aeroprakt A32, Jabiru, Foxbat, Sling, and similar types. RAAus aircraft typically have a maximum take-off weight of 600 kg, though some categories go higher.

An RPL is limited to single-engine aircraft with a maximum take-off weight of 1500 kg or less. That covers most common GA trainers, including the Cessna 152, Cessna 172, Piper Warrior, Piper Archer, and Diamond DA40. It does not cover heavier aircraft like the Cessna 182. To fly a multi-engine aeroplane, you would need a PPL with a multi-engine class rating.

In practical terms, the aircraft available at your local schools may influence your decision. If your nearest school operates RAAus aircraft, the RPC might be the more accessible starting point. If your nearest school operates Cessnas, Pipers, or Diamonds, the RPL may be the natural pathway. Many schools offer both.

## Passengers

Passenger privileges are one of the areas where people get confused. The rules are not complicated, but they depend on your medical class, not just your licence.

For an RPC, RAAus has a separate **Passenger Carrying endorsement**. You do not get to carry passengers automatically when you finish your RPC. You need to complete additional pilot-in-command time, complete the required training, and demonstrate safe proficiency. Until then, you fly solo.

For an RPL, the rule is tied to your medical:

- **RPL with a recreational medical** (RAMPC or Class 5 self-declaration): one passenger maximum.
- **RPL with a Class 2 medical or higher**: more than one passenger allowed, up to the aircraft's seating capacity.
- **RPL with a recreational medical, but accompanied by another pilot in a control seat who holds a Class 2 or higher and is authorised to fly the aircraft**: more than one passenger allowed.

The takeaway is that passenger privileges are not automatic in the simple "I have a qualification, so I can take everyone flying" sense. Your medical, endorsements, aircraft, and operating rules all affect what you can actually do.

## Navigation and flying away from the local area

Both pathways start local. To fly cross-country, you need additional training and an endorsement.

RAAus has a **Cross Country endorsement** for pilots who want to travel beyond 25 nautical miles from their departure aerodrome.

CASA has a **Recreational Navigation endorsement** for RPL holders, which requires passing the RPL Navigation exam and meeting minimum flight time requirements including solo cross-country time.

This matters because many beginners imagine that once they have their first qualification, they can immediately fly all over Australia. In reality, your initial privileges are limited to within 25 nautical miles of your departure aerodrome, your training area, and the route between them. Cross-country flying requires additional training and authorisation.

That is not a bad thing. Navigation training is one of the most enjoyable and useful parts of learning to fly. It is often where flying starts to feel less like circuits around the airport and more like genuine travel.

## Controlled airspace

Controlled airspace is another important difference to understand.

If you train at a regional or country aerodrome outside controlled airspace, you may not think about it often. If you train near a major city or a controlled airport, it may become relevant much sooner.

For RPL holders, additional endorsements include controlled aerodrome, controlled airspace, flight radio, and recreational navigation endorsements. Each requires its own training and assessment.

For RAAus pilots, controlled airspace access has its own rules and requirements. You should get current advice from RAAus and your flight school before assuming what is possible.

For a beginner, the simple version is this:

**If you want or need to operate in controlled airspace, talk to your school early. Do not assume your basic qualification automatically gives you access.**

## Medical requirements

Medical requirements can strongly influence your pathway, and this is one area that has changed significantly in recent years.

For an **RPC**, RAAus uses a self-declared medical based on the standard required for a private motor vehicle driver's licence. You declare your fitness yourself, without needing to see a specialist aviation doctor.

For an **RPL**, you have several options, in roughly increasing order of complexity and cost:

- **Class 5 medical self-declaration**: Introduced in February 2024, this allows recreational and private pilots to self-assess and self-declare without seeing a doctor. You complete an online module, submit an application through myCASA, and pay a small fee. It is the simplest and cheapest CASA medical option. Validity is 5 years for pilots aged 16 to 39, 2 years for pilots aged 40 to 74, and 1 year for pilots 75 and over.
- **Recreational Aviation Medical Practitioner's Certificate (RAMPC)**: A medical certificate issued by your GP using a CASA-approved process. Cheaper and simpler than a Class 2.
- **Basic Class 2 medical**: Issued by any doctor who performs commercial driver medicals, using the unconditional Austroads commercial driver standard. Limited to private day VFR operations below 10,000 feet.
- **Class 2 medical**: Issued by a CASA Designated Aviation Medical Examiner (DAME). Required for full RPL privileges including flying above 10,000 feet and carrying multiple passengers.

If you are flying purely recreationally, the Class 5 self-declaration is now the path of least resistance for most people. If you want to become a commercial pilot, you should think about medical requirements much earlier, because commercial aviation requires a Class 1 medical and the standards are more demanding. There is no point spending tens of thousands of dollars on training only to discover you cannot get a Class 1.

A good rule:

**If flying is just for fun, understand the medical requirements before you start. If flying is your future career, understand them before you spend serious money.**

## Cost and training environment

The RPC is often seen as the lower-cost entry point. That is generally true, because RAAus aircraft are typically cheaper to hire and operate than traditional GA aircraft. The hourly rate difference can be $100 to $150 per hour or more.

However, cost should not be assessed only by hourly aircraft rate.

A cheaper aircraft does not always mean cheaper training overall. The quality of instruction, school organisation, aircraft availability, weather, your consistency, and how often you fly all affect the final cost. A school with a slightly cheaper aircraft is not cheaper if you spend extra hours repeating lessons because the training is disorganised.

The RPL may cost more, especially in larger GA aircraft, but it may align better with your future goals if you know you want to continue through the CASA system.

The real question is not "which one is cheaper?" A better question is:

**Which pathway gets me to my actual goal with the least wasted time, money, and duplicated training?**

## Can you start with an RPC and move to an RPL later?

Yes, and many pilots do exactly that.

CASA treats an RAAus pilot certificate as equivalent to an RPL. Your hours and many of your endorsements transfer across. To convert, you complete an application form, provide evidence of your pilot certificate, and complete a flight review in a VH-registered aircraft.

The cost saving can be significant. Training for an RPC in a recreational aircraft and then converting to an RPL is often cheaper overall than training for an RPL from scratch in a GA aircraft. This is one of the main reasons many flight schools recommend the RAAus pathway as a starting point, even for students who eventually want to fly commercially.

If you think you may eventually want a PPL, CPL, or professional aviation career, tell your school that from the beginning. A good instructor or head of operations can help you avoid unnecessary duplication.

## Which one should you choose?

Choose the **RPC** if your main goal is affordable recreational flying, especially in light sport or recreational aircraft, and your nearest school operates RAAus aircraft.

Choose the **RPL** if you want to train in the CASA system, fly GA aircraft, or continue toward a PPL or CPL.

Consider starting with an **RPC and transitioning later** if the RAAus pathway is available locally, cost is a major factor, and you are happy to begin in recreational aircraft while keeping future options open.

Still unsure? That is normal.

The best thing you can do is speak to two or three local schools. Ask them what aircraft they use, what pathway they recommend, what the realistic costs are, and how your training would credit if you later changed direction.

## Questions to ask a flight school

Before choosing between an RPC and RPL, ask:

- Do you offer RPC, RPL, or both?
- What aircraft would I train in?
- What is the realistic total cost, not just the minimum cost?
- How often should I fly to progress well?
- What privileges will I have at the end?
- Can I carry passengers, and under what conditions?
- Can I fly cross-country?
- Can I operate in controlled airspace?
- What medical do I need, and which option do you recommend?
- If I continue toward a PPL or CPL, how much of this training will count?
- What are the common reasons students get delayed?

These questions will tell you a lot about both the pathway and the school.

## Final thoughts

If your goal is weekend recreational flying, the RPC may be perfect. If your goal is a long-term aviation career, the RPL may be a better fit. If you are still unsure, the most useful thing you can do is take a trial flight at both an RAAus school and a GA school if possible.

The best pathway is the one that matches your goal, your budget, your location, and the kind of flying you actually want to do.
